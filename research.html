Complex coacervates

Polyelectrolyte complexation occurs through electrostatic association between oppositely charged polymers. Depending on composition and solution conditions, this association can lead to liquid–liquid phase separation, forming liquid-like coacervates. These coacervate materials provide a versatile platform for controlling molecular partitioning, reaction environments, and interfacial properties.

We investigate the principles governing polyelectrolyte complexation and apply them to compartmentalized systems, hydrogel design, selective separations, and the development of functional soft materials.

Coacervate Protocells and Membraneless Compartments

High-throughput design of compartmentalized micro-bioreactors

Biological cells organize biochemical processes through membrane-bound organelles and membraneless compartments. Complex coacervation offers a synthetic route to compartmentalization by forming polymer-rich and polymer-lean phases that selectively concentrate biomolecules and facilitate bioreactions.

We invented an approach to mitigate coalescence of complex coacervate droplets using comb polyelectrolytes as an effective stabilizer. These membraneless assemblies provide months-long stability without impacting molecular transport and selective partitioning of species. This approach overcomes a conventional limitation of phase-separated systems, which often suffer from coalescence and macrophase separation. We also develop high-throughput methods for quantitative characterization of these dispersions.

Coacervate compartments provide a means of organizing cell-free enzymatic reactions within a single vessel. By selectively partitioning enzymes and artificial redox cofactors, these compartments can establish distinct reaction environments and influence the kinetics of multienzyme cascades. Our work, in collaboration with the Mittal Group on coarse-grained modeling of stabilized coacervate dispersions and the Abel Group on modeling kinetic reactions in phase-separated systems, provides key insights into stabilization mechanisms and microenvironment tuning for their use as efficient bioreactors.

We are developing coacervate-based systems for redox biocatalysis and cofactor recycling, targeting industrial applications such as biocatalysis and enantio-enriched chiral products. Our current work examines selective transport across coacervate interfaces, stimulus-responsive permeability, droplet fusion and division, and the spatial organization of multienzyme reactions. These studies address the design principles needed to control molecular exchange and biochemical activity in synthetic membraneless compartments.

Bio-inspired Coacervate Hydrogel Scaffolds and Adhesives

Polyelectrolyte complex hydrogels as scaffolds

Block polyelectrolytes of opposite charge associate in aqueous media to form hydrogels in which the network junctions are electrostatic complexes rather than covalent crosslinks. We have investigated how block architecture, charge density, and ionic strength determine the structure and mechanical response of these networks.

We established polyelectrolyte complex–covalent interpenetrating networks (PEC-IPNs), in which a physically associated complex network interpenetrates a covalently crosslinked one, and characterized their network formation. Using small-angle X-ray scattering and rheometry, we resolved the microstructural rearrangements that occur in triblock polyelectrolyte complex hydrogels during processing and under deformation and related these rearrangements to the observed viscoelastic response.

We have further employed polyelectrolyte complexes to modulate the processing behavior of aqueous formulations. Addition of block polyelectrolytes to gelatin methacryloyl inks altered their viscoelasticity sufficiently to permit extrusion-based 3D printing at physiological temperature. We have also demonstrated that complex scaffoldings template photo-crosslinked hydrogels in aqueous media, and we characterized the structure, swelling, dissolution, and protein-release behavior of complex hydrogels used as delivery depots.

Current work addresses injectable formulations that retain their geometry after placement, and the development of structure–property relationships linking block architecture to network microstructure and release kinetics.

Bio-inspired wet adhesives

Adhesion in aqueous environments is limited by the competition of water for the interactions that bond a polymer to a surface. Marine organisms, including mussels and sandcastle worms, achieve underwater adhesion by secreting catechol-containing proteins that spread on wet surfaces prior to curing.

Drawing on this chemistry, we synthesized water-soluble block polymers bearing catechol functionalities and examined how molecular architecture and adhesive-moiety placement govern interfacial and mechanical properties. We developed a modular synthetic route to catechol-functionalized triblock copolymers and demonstrated rapid network formation upon catechol oxidation while retaining adhesive functionality, allowing us to tune cohesive and interfacial contributions separately. In collaboration with the Whitmer Group, we have shown that transfer learning predicts polymer–surface adhesion strength from molecular descriptors, reducing the synthetic effort required to identify candidate adhesives. Ongoing work addresses adhesives that cure on demand in wet environments and the combination of PEC-IPN network mechanics with catechol-mediated adhesion in a single material.

Fundamentals of Complex Coacervation

Phase behavior and thermodynamics

Electrostatic interactions and solution conditions govern polyelectrolyte complexation, determining phase behavior, complex composition, and material dynamics. We construct phase diagrams and examine how salt partitioning, solvent quality, and non-electrostatic interactions contribute to complexation. Our study contrasts the influence of multivalent ions on complexation with that of compositional and length asymmetry on coacervate properties. By combining experiments with molecular dynamics simulations, we were able to decouple the effect of electrostatic interactions from non-electrostatic contributors such as pH, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobicity. In collaboration with the Caram Group and the Whitmer Group, we investigate how non-interacting species affect the phase diagram and the composition of the bulk phases.

Composition and relaxation dynamics

Our work connects phase behavior with the composition and relaxation dynamics of polyelectrolyte complexes. We introduced time–ionic strength superposition to unify linear viscoelastic measurements across salt concentrations. We investigate how divalent ions and compositional asymmetry influence chain relaxation dynamics.

Smart Water Treatment and Selective Separation Technologies

Reliable access to clean water requires treatment systems that can adapt to changing water quality while remaining practical for small communities with limited technical resources. We develop distributed water treatment technologies that combine process intensification, modular design, and automation to support decentralized operation and reduce dependence on skilled personnel.

In collaboration with the Cohen Group and the Christofides Group, we integrate physicochemical treatment processes with membrane-based separations to develop compact, modular treatment systems suited to community-scale deployment. Process intensification and automation aim to improve treatment efficiency and simplify operation, while adaptive control strategies enable systems to respond to changes in feedwater quality. The primary motivation is to make robust water treatment more accessible to small communities through adaptable, automated, and decentralized technologies.

Beyond these efforts, we are developing selective separation platforms to recover rare-earth and heavy-metal ions from industrial streams and extract proteins from fermentation broths. We investigate how molecular recognition, ion desolvation, metal speciation, and receptor placement govern ion selectivity and reversible binding. In parallel, we explore aqueous two-phase systems and complex coacervates for protein extraction, examining how external stimuli influence molecular partitioning to enable selective recovery and regeneration under controlled operating conditions.

Interfaces, Thin Films, and Dense Emulsions

Thin films and intermicellar interactions

Ultrathin foam films provide a platform for investigating surfactant organization under confinement. By combining measurements of film stratification, in collaboration with the Sharma Group, with small-angle X-ray scattering, we relate nanoscale film structure to micellar organization and intermolecular interactions. We also examine how salt concentration influences micellar interactions and film stratification.

Imaging and modeling of dense emulsions

In dense oil-in-water emulsions, closely packed droplets deform and interact through thin, surfactant-stabilized films. In collaboration with the Peterson Group, we use controlled-flow experiments and high-speed microscopy to characterize droplet deformation, rearrangement, interactions, and breakup. Machine-learning-assisted image analysis enables quantitative tracking of these dynamics across many droplets, supporting the development of relationships between local droplet behavior, emulsion structure, and macroscopic flow.

Sustainable Composites and Cements

Upcycling post-consumer plastics

We investigate practical approaches for sorting post-consumer plastics using observable material properties, linking polymer composition to chemical and thermomechanical behavior. We then chemically recycle selected plastics and upcycle the resulting products into high-value organic–inorganic composites with enhanced mechanical and functional properties. This approach explores routes to convert plastic waste into functional materials while developing recycling strategies that adapt to diverse plastic compositions.

Low-carbon printable binders

Portlandite-based cementitious suspensions can sequester carbon dioxide through mineralization during curing. Their use in printing requires control over particle dispersion, flow, and setting. In collaboration with the Sant Group and the UCLA Institute for Carbon Management, we investigate electrosteric interactions that govern portlandite dispersion and aggregation, as well as thermoresponsive polymer additives that enable rapid, triggered stiffening. This work extends to carbon dioxide mineralization with active control of setting and the mechanisms by which graphene oxide reinforces cementitious materials.
