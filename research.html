Complex coacervates

Design · assembly · structure · function

Complex coacervation is straightforward to observe and difficult to predict. Whether liquid–liquid phase separation arrests at ten nanometers or ten micrometers, whether the resulting coacervate behaves as a viscous liquid or an elastic solid, and how rapidly it relaxes under stress are determined by molecular details — block architecture, charge density and sequence, counterion valency, chain length, and solvent quality — whose individual effects are modest and whose combined effect is decisive.

We treat these as design variables. The four research areas below share a common basis in polyelectrolyte complexation and differ in what is constructed from it.

Coacervate protocells and membraneless compartments

Cells organize chemistry in space, and a substantial part of that organization is membraneless: proteins and nucleic acids condense by liquid–liquid phase separation into droplets that concentrate certain species, exclude others, and disperse when they are no longer required. Complex coacervates are the synthetic analogue of such compartments and among the most plausible laboratory models of a prebiotic cell.

Their principal limitation has been stability. Coacervate droplets coalesce, and a dispersion of microdroplets typically becomes a single bulk phase within minutes. A compartment that cannot maintain a boundary cannot function as a cell.

Our contributions

We have shown that coalescence can be arrested without a membrane. Comb polyelectrolytes adsorbed at the coacervate–water interface stabilize microdroplets through macromolecular assembly alone, yielding dispersions — complex coacervate emulsions — that remain stable for weeks, with droplet size and salt tolerance set by comb polyelectrolyte concentration. We subsequently developed quantitative turbidimetric methods for characterizing such dispersions, allowing droplet populations to be measured rather than only observed.

Function

Stabilized coacervates act as colloidal bioreactors. They sequester proteins and enzymes at concentrations well above those of the surrounding solution, and co-encapsulation of multiple enzymes alters cascade reaction kinetics relative to bulk. Earlier work established that actin partitions into polypeptide coacervates and assembles more readily within them, indicating that the coacervate interior constitutes a distinct chemical environment rather than simply a concentrated solution.

Cell-free biomanufacturing

The most immediate application of a stabilized coacervate compartment is chemical rather than prebiotic. Cell-free enzymatic cascades avoid the competing background chemistry of a living cell, but in doing so they give up the spatial organization that allows a cell to run opposed reactions simultaneously; in a conventional one-pot system, distinct local redox environments cannot be maintained. We are developing membraneless coacervate droplets that encapsulate enzymes together with artificial redox cofactors in spatially separated compartments, so that reducing and oxidizing chemistry can proceed in a single vessel with efficient cofactor recycling. The proving targets are enantio-enriched chiral amines and alcohols, whose conventional synthesis is limited by production cost and by the difficulty of chiral separation. This work is carried out as a multi-institution academic–industry collaboration running through 2028.

Current directions

Alongside the biomanufacturing work, present efforts address selective and stimulus-gated transport across the coacervate interface, controlled division and fusion, the spatial organization of multi-enzyme cascades, and exchange of material between droplet populations — the elements required of a protocell that performs a function rather than merely persisting.

Bio-inspired adhesives and coacervate hydrogel scaffolds

Adhesive bonding in water is difficult, and load-bearing hydrogels are difficult for a related reason: water competes for the interactions that hold the material together, and covalent networks that are stiff are generally brittle while those that are extensible are generally weak.

Marine organisms address this problem differently. Mussels and sandcastle worms secrete adhesive proteins in a coacervate state — dense fluids of low interfacial tension that spread on wet surfaces before curing — with catechol groups providing interfacial attachment. The design principles worth borrowing are the catechol chemistry that grips a wet surface and the ability to build cohesion after placement — which we pursue through polymer synthesis rather than by reproducing the coacervate delivery route itself.

Our contributions

We have shown that block polyelectrolytes assemble into micellar network hydrogels whose structure and mechanical response are set by architecture as much as by chemistry, including gelation in the dilute regime where conventional networks do not form. Combining the electrostatic network with a covalent one produces polyelectrolyte complex–covalent interpenetrating networks in which shear and tensile strength increase together while the self-assembled microstructure is preserved, providing a route around the usual trade-off between strength and toughness. More recent work has resolved the microstructural rearrangements these triblock networks undergo under deformation and ageing, and has established a modular synthesis of catechol-functionalized triblock copolymers that introduces mussel-inspired interfacial chemistry into a self-assembling architecture.

Applications

Block polyelectrolyte additives modify the viscoelasticity of gelatin inks sufficiently to permit three-dimensional printing at physiological temperature, addressing a persistent constraint in bioprinting. Polyelectrolyte complex scaffoldings serve as templates for photocrosslinked hydrogels, and polyelectrolyte complex hydrogels function as protein depots whose swelling, dissolution, and release characteristics we have quantified. Collaborative work using transfer learning allows polymer–surface adhesion strength to be predicted prior to synthesis.

Current directions

Work in progress concerns adhesives that cure on demand in wet tissue, injectable scaffolds that retain geometry after placement, and a predictive relationship between block architecture and adhesive performance. A sustained effort in the group is directed at durable wet adhesives for wound closure and implant fixation.

Fundamentals of complex coacervation

The work described in the other research areas rests on this one. Polyelectrolyte complexes are strongly correlated, sensitive to salt, rich in water, and slow to relax — a combination that resists simple theoretical description. The field has consequently been described largely case by case, for a given polymer pair, salt, and pH. Rational design requires a more general account.

Our contributions — relaxation dynamics

We introduced time–ionic strength superposition, which shows that the linear viscoelastic response of polyelectrolyte complexes measured at different salt concentrations collapses onto a single master curve. Salt accelerates chain relaxation in a manner analogous to temperature in polymer melts. The construction renders an otherwise inaccessible long-time regime measurable and provides a unified description of chain relaxation across complex compositions.

Our contributions — phase behavior and thermodynamics

We have measured salt partitioning between coacervate and supernatant phases, established the effect of solvent quality on the binodal, and separated the contributions of non-electrostatic interactions — hydrogen bonding, hydrophobicity, and pH-dependent charge — from purely Coulombic ones, showing that electrostatics rarely acts alone in these systems. Divalent counterions are found to alter both the composition and the viscoelasticity of the complex phase, which is consequential wherever natural or industrial water is involved.

Our contributions — structure and methodology

We have developed and documented small-angle X-ray scattering approaches for resolving macromolecular and self-assembled polyelectrolyte complexes, and we work with simulation groups on explicit-solvent models that reproduce coacervate structure and thermodynamics without treating water as a dielectric continuum.

Current directions

Present work concerns sequence-level control. Charge patterning along the chain, chirality, and architecture modulate complexation in ways not captured by mean-field treatments. The objective is a design relationship connecting sequence to phase diagram and relaxation spectrum.

Intelligent water treatment and selective separation technologies

Conventional water treatment is deliberately unselective: coagulation, flocculation, filtration, disposal. This is appropriate for potable water and inadequate for a growing set of problems — recovery of lithium from brine, removal of a specific heavy metal from an industrial stream, isolation of a protein from a fermentation broth, and treatment of produced water of variable composition.

Selectivity requires materials that discriminate between species differing only in charge, size, or hydration. Polyelectrolytes and coacervate phases do so, and their selectivity is adjustable through the same design variables examined in our work on coacervation fundamentals.

Our contributions

We have set out the physical basis of polyelectrolyte coagulation and flocculation — the roles of chain architecture, charge density, and ionic environment in bridging, patch neutralization, and floc structure — placing a largely empirical industrial practice on a polymer-physics footing. We have resolved the structure of nanoparticle–polyelectrolyte complexes as a function of polyelectrolyte characteristics and charge ratio, establishing how a charged polymer organizes around a colloidal contaminant. Our review of interfacial stabilization in aqueous two-phase systems sets out the design space for liquid–liquid separations requiring no organic solvent, and work on structure-directing cations in zeolite synthesis addresses selective sorbent design from the inorganic side. Results on divalent counterions bear directly on this work, since real feed streams contain calcium and magnesium in quantity.

Work underway

Two applications are being pursued directly. The recovery of lithium from brine requires discriminating a small, strongly hydrated cation from a large excess of chemically similar ions, and the treatment of produced water requires doing so in a feed whose composition varies with time. Both are selectivity problems rather than removal problems, and both depend on the divalent-counterion behavior established in our fundamentals work, since real feed streams carry calcium and magnesium in quantity.

A complementary approach: optimizing the pretreatment train

Selective media are one route to better separations. Operating existing infrastructure better is another, and we pursue both.

Reverse osmosis dominates desalination and water reuse, and its performance is governed largely by what arrives at the membrane. Suspended solids, colloidal matter, organic foulants, and scale-forming salts cause fouling, scaling, and premature membrane damage, so pretreatment trains are designed conservatively — sized and dosed for the worst feed a plant expects rather than the feed it is receiving at any moment. The energy penalty and chemical consumption of that conservatism are substantial.

We are developing machine-learning-based optimization of the pretreatment train, treating the sequence of coagulation, filtration, and antiscalant addition, together with dosing and operating set points, as a system to be tuned against a feed whose composition varies with time. The aim is reduced specific energy consumption and higher water recovery without new hardware.

This work is data-driven rather than coacervate science, but the two meet at a specific point. Our account of polyelectrolyte coagulation and flocculation — bridging, patch neutralization, and floc structure as functions of chain architecture, charge density, and ionic environment — provides the mechanistic understanding of what a coagulant dose is actually doing to a colloidal contaminant. The learned model supplies what mechanism alone cannot: adaptation to a feed that will not hold still. Mechanistic insight constrains the design space; optimization searches it.

Current directions

Beyond these, we are extending the platform toward selective heavy-metal capture from industrial streams and toward aqueous two-phase and coacervate extraction of proteins from fermentation broth in place of solvent-based processes. The objective in each case is separation media whose partition coefficients can be altered during operation — by salinity, pH, temperature, or applied field — and regenerated in place, giving a separations platform specified from molecular design rather than arrived at empirically.

Collaborative direction · with the Sharma and Peterson groups

Interfaces, thin films, and dense emulsions

Several of the research areas above depend on the behavior of interfaces only a few molecules thick, and on what happens when droplets are packed closely enough that those interfaces dominate the response of the material.

Thin films and intermicellar interactions · with the Sharma group

Foam film stratification, combined with X-ray scattering, probes intermicellar interactions directly, resolving how surfactant micelles order under confinement and how added salt weakens that ordering. The same measurements inform coacervate droplet stabilization, flotation-based separations, and the stability of formulated products.

Imaging and modeling of dense emulsions · with the Peterson group

The thin films that separate bubbles in a foam also separate droplets in a concentrated emulsion, and much the same surfactant physics governs both. In a dense oil-in-water emulsion the droplets are pressed into contact and deform against one another, held apart by surfactant-stabilized films only nanometres thick. The stratification and intermicellar ordering we resolve in foam films therefore bear directly on how such an emulsion yields and flows, and the emulsion supplies a second, geometrically distinct setting in which to test the same understanding.

We image these emulsions directly and apply machine-learning-aided image analysis to segment and track individual droplets across large image volumes and over time. Automated analysis makes population-level quantities accessible — size distributions, coordination numbers, contact areas, and rearrangement events — that are impractical to extract by hand. The objective is a description that connects the behavior of a single film to the flow and failure of the emulsion as a whole.

Collaborative area · with the Sant group and the UCLA Institute for Carbon Management

Sustainable composites and cements

Construction materials are colloidal suspensions before they are solids, and their processability is governed by the electrosteric interactions that also determine behavior in aqueous polyelectrolyte systems. We apply this perspective to two problems.

Low-carbon printable binders

Portlandite-based cements sequester carbon dioxide as they cure, provided the suspension can be dispersed, extruded, and set on command. We have established how electrosteric exclusion disperses portlandite particulates at short screening lengths, how temperature drives aggregation, and how thermoresponsive polymer additives produce rapid, triggered stiffening — the rheological basis for printing a carbon-sequestering binder. Recent work extends this to carbon dioxide mineralization with active set control and to the mechanism of graphene oxide reinforcement in cementitious matrices.

Upcycling of post-consumer plastics

We chemically recycle post-consumer polyurethane foam and combine the glycolyzed product with naturally occurring zeolite minerals to produce organic–inorganic composites whose tensile and compressive strengths exceed those of cement, with favorable thermal insulation and acoustic attenuation. We also develop field-applicable sorting strategies that relate readily observable physical properties to the thermomechanical and chemical properties governing depolymerization.
